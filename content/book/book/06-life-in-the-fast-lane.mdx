In 1976 the Eagles released "Life in the Fast Lane" as part of their hit album Hotel California, the same year I was born. Glenn Frey got the title from an actual car ride, a guy nicknamed The Count who was doing close to 90 in the left lane with no signs of slowing down. Frey told him to ease off. The guy said back, "this is life in the fast lane". Frey knew a title when he heard one.

<img src="/book-images/fastlane-left.png" width="873" alt="" />

I must have heard that song hundreds of times growing up. It opens in an exhilarating way, then spends the rest of it showing you what happens once speed becomes the primary goal. With the Givergy acquisition we didn't get a choice about the speed. The only real question was whether we could move fast enough without losing control. While the fast lane in the Eagles song was all about speed without restraint ours had to run on speed with intelligence. After all, speed without restraint is almost always destructive.

### The Fast Lane Agent

Once Givergy's product came online, product requests started arriving from two organizations at once instead of one, and every single one of them needed the same thing before anyone could act on it - is this real, and if it's real, where does it go. That triage used to be a person's job - read the request, go check a few systems, form an opinion, route it. It worked fine at our old volume. At the combined volume, it was the bottleneck. Requests piled up waiting on someone to have a free minute, and the ones that got triaged fastest weren't necessarily the ones that mattered most - they were just whoever's request landed on a quiet afternoon.

That's the problem the Fastlane agent exists to solve. Instead of a person doing that first pass by hand, Fastlane does it the moment a request comes in - gathering evidence, weighing how strong that evidence actually is, and only acting on it once it's confident enough to trust. We didn't build a new search layer to make that possible. We leaned on the same search tools we'd already built for Ask Product, which meant Fastlane could start doing real work almost immediately instead of reinventing something we already had working.

Adding real analysis into the product request pipeline itself, so requests coming in fast could actually get seen, classified, and routed correctly while they were still moving, instead of getting sorted out later once things had already piled up.

Curiously, the opening guitar riff in Life in the Fastlane wasn't written as a song. Joe Walsh was warming up, Frey heard a few seconds of it and recognized there something real. They didn't spend months designing the perfect structure. Somebody recognized what was there, built around it and kept things moving. That's closer to how we actually built the fastlane agent than I'd like to admit, but sometimes innovation tends to work that way. Being able to lean into the process and sometimes just let things rip.

“Life in the fast lane, everything all the time.”

Interestingly enough, the album is about what happens after success arrives. The Eagles were themselves scaling while making an album about the consequences of scaling. Their greatest hits record was becoming enormously successful while they were recording Hotel California.

Producer Bill Szymczyk candidly remarked, “While it was great to be leading the way, there was also the pressure to keep producing the hits.” Success creates opportunity, and along with opportunity comes expectations. This is when things start to really accelerate.

“Life in the fast lane, surely make you lose your mind.”

Scaling requires forthought.

Finding the right information was only useful if we could do something with it. The evidence gave us a picture of the problem, but the next step was to act on what we found. A duplicate could be closed. A real bug could move forward. A missing piece of context could be requested. The connections we were making were beginning to have consequences.
