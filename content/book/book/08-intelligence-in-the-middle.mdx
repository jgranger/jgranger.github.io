When I was in college, I wanted to take as many computing classes as I could. I was already working full time as a software developer, opportunity was everywhere and electives initially felt like something standing between me and the subjects I thought mattered most. But I was required to take them, and something unexpected happened. I loved them. Looking back some of those electives turned out to be among the most valuable classes I took.

Cognitive psychology was one of them. I became fascinated by how intelligence could emerge from an enormous network of interconnected neurons, none particularly intelligent on its own. I was interested enough that I briefly considered pursuing it further, but my career was already moving quickly in another direction. That interest in connected systems found a practical outlet in distributed computing. I spent years working on distributed systems where disparate nodes were coordinated to work together. Distributed systems don't become more intelligent simply because we connect them, but they do become more resilient, scalable and performant. Work can be distributed across nodes, failures can be isolated and the collective system can accomplish things that would overwhelm any single instance. The value isn't contained in any one node. It comes from how the system is connected and coordinated.

Decades later, those paths have converged. Cognitive psychology gave me an early glimpse of intelligence emerging from a network. Distributed computing taught me what happens when we deliberately engineer systems of connected nodes. Agentic systems bring the two together. The nodes can now reason, and when we connect them, something far more interesting can happen in the middle.
### The Opportunity

People, more than agents bring a lifetime of contextual awareness and product knowledge. People show up, correct, and help out of curiosity - those are the people who'll excel in an AI infused world. Every chapter so far has demonstrated in some way intelligence in the middle of a process - a model reasoning over the right context instead of a person doing all the fetching and filtering by hand. This chapter is about the other half of that. People are still in the middle too, and if anything they matter more now, not less. A human brain is a huge network of small, specialized things constantly passing messages to each other, each one holding a little context the others don't have. Our organization runs the same way. People pass each other messages - an answer, a correction, a screenshot, a "wait, that's not right" and the organization gets smarter to the degree those messages carry real context instead of noise.

Showing up when it matters most is still the single most valuable thing a person can do - bringing the right piece of context, at the moment someone else needs it. I've watched people who'd only been half paying attention to an Ask Product conversation jump in unprompted to correct something the agent got wrong, or add the one piece of context that made an ambiguous answer make sense. Nobody assigned them that job. They just had something worth contributing and the moment called for it.

That shift shows up in how people outside engineering actually use these agents. Salespeople talking to Ask Product weren't just asking questions for themselves - they were surfacing, in real time, exactly where the product's documentation didn't match reality, or where a capability a customer wanted didn't exist yet. That's product feedback showing up faster and more honestly than a roadmap review, because it's coming out of an actual conversation with a customer.

People also don't always show up speaking the way you'd expect. Someone typing in shorthand, half a sentence, straight-up sales-floor slang - that's exactly what they'd give a coworker who already knows the context. Whether the agent can meet a person where they are, instead of making them translate themselves first is a real test of whether the thing is actually useful. AI is leverage for the people who've figured out how to be excellent at the part only they can do - showing up, bringing the right context, using judgment nobody automated away. Those are the people who get more valuable as the agents around them get more capable.
### Self Replication

Satya Nadella, the CEO of Microsoft said something recently that really resonated with me. I'm paraphrasing, but here's what I heard. "All of us are going to be managers, managers of infinite minds."

Building loops, graphs and processes aren't just for engineers. Not any longer anyways. We're at a period in history where everyone has access to elite levels of intelligence and tools to accomplish what to many is becoming indistinguishable from magic. The most powerful and important reality to understand is we have all of these things at scale. What we choose to do with them is completely up to us. I believe the opportunity of a lifetime is set before us, and the opportunity is self-replication.

Consider your unique talents and abilities, how you think, what you notice, your key information sources, the tools you use, your judgment, and the things you're unusually good at. Then begin encoding those characteristics into agents. You are effectively constructing a digital extension of yourself. Not one generic assistant, but a collection of specialized agents representing different capabilities and perspectives that previously existed only inside you.

The metaphor expands from a graph into a universe. Your capabilities, agents, tools, and information sources become your own solar system or galaxy - many independent bodies operating in relationship with one another. They don't all communicate with everything else. The meaningful connections matter. There are hundreds of billions of stars in the known universe. The digital universe has effectively unlimited undeclared territory. You can create as many specialized intelligences as are useful and much of this territory has not yet been defined, named or claimed.

That creates a provocative idea around identity - this undeclared digital territory can be something upon which you stake your identity. Instead of merely consuming AI created by others you define your own constellation of intelligence - an ecosystem that increasingly embodies your knowledge, abilities, judgment, and perspective. The future isn't one increasingly powerful artificial mind. It's networks of differentiated minds whose relationships produce capabilities none of them possess individually. Graph engineering provides the structure. Operational context provides the right information. Agents provide specialized intelligence. And self-replication lets the individual become the architect of an entire intelligence ecosystem.

At some point you have to decide where you're going to live. You can stay in the Google Galaxy - a familiar, well-lit place where you type the query, scan the ten blue links, do all the filtering and reasoning yourself. It's worked for twenty five years. Nobody will fault you for staying there. Or, you can step into the agentic universe - a much bigger, less orderly place where intelligence doesn't wait for you to ask the right question. It searches, filters, reasons and acts on your behalf, across a constellation of specialized agents built in your own image. It asks more of you up front - you have to actually define who you are, what you know and what you're good at before any of it can be encoded. But it's also the only place where you get to scale yourself.
