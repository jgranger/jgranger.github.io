The bigger structural shift was happening underneath us, and not because we asked for it. We'd been building against Mastra Cloud, which was a Beta offerring we were an early adoper of. We were running multiple production agents on a Beta platform, and it had been rebuilt from the ground up as the Mastra Platform and was about to be released. The migration had to go perfectly. 

The migration changed some of the assumptions we'd quietly been relying on, and we had to adapt some of what we'd built around those assumptions. But it landed us somewhere more reliable and more scalable than where we started, and a real part of why it worked was how closely we ended up working with the Mastra team through it. They treated us the way we treat our customers, which is they showed up with care when it really mattered. That's not a small thing. Technology partnerships either have that alignment or they don't, and you find out which one you've got right when something breaks mid-migration.

I remember talking to my teammate Adriano and we were going back and forth about whether we should host our own agents on our own infrastructure. We went back and forth a few times and ultimately settled on the reality that our number one goal isn't to build infrastructure it's to help transform the business.  And yet relying on a third party for what ultimately you are responsible for is not a trivial consideration. Passing the buck and saying the problem lives upstream and there's nothing we can do about it was simply not an option for us. It doesn't align with our values and it doesn't align with how our customers perceive us. It's certainly doesn't align with how we see ourselves and if we were going to take a risk with a new cloud provider we had to be certain they shared the same values that we did for both short term and long term success.

As we were innovating we were building on infrastructure that was itself still being built, alongside the people building it. That's a different experience than adopting a finished platform off a shelf. You're exposed to more churn, but you're also close enough to shape what the finished thing looks like, and close enough to a team who, for a while, was solving our specific problems instead of some generic customer's.

We went from having a few agents to having a platform tailored to our needs. We had processes, skills, and guardrails in place that would allows us to scale. Life was good. 

One of the first things we learned moving agents into production is that getting an agent to work is only the beginning. You can build something surprisingly capable in a short period of time. The real engineering begins when people start depending on it. At that point the prompt becomes a relatively small part of a much larger system. Reliability, evaluation, observability, latency, cost, infrastructure and all of the dependencies surrounding the model suddenly matter.

Agents also fail differently than traditional software. An application can crash, throw an exception or return an error code. An agent can execute perfectly and still be wrong. Everything can be green. The request succeeds, the model responds and the application behaves exactly as designed, yet the result can still be bad. That changes what production reliability means. It isn't enough to know that the agent is running. You have to know whether it is actually doing its job.

This changed the way I thought about evaluation. With traditional software we spend enormous amounts of energy proving that the machinery works. With agents we also have to evaluate the behavior coming out of the machinery. Uncertainty is part of the system. We can't engineer it away, but we can engineer around it. Evals, evidence, confidence, fallbacks and human judgment become part of the architecture. The more consequential the agent becomes, the more important those surrounding systems become.

We also learned that the model itself is a dependency, and a particularly unusual one. A newer model can be better by almost every conventional measure and still be worse at something your agent depends on. A model change can alter behavior without changing a single line of your own code. The same is true of prompts, retrieval strategies and the tools surrounding the model. Everything has to be treated as part of the system because changing any one of those pieces can change the behavior of the whole.

As our agents became more sophisticated, another lesson became increasingly clear. Production agents are systems, not prompts. They search, retrieve, route, call tools, evaluate results, make decisions and sometimes hand work to other agents. Some operations happen sequentially because one decision determines what happens next. Others happen in parallel because there is no reason to wait. Some paths terminate quickly while others expand into deeper investigation. The intelligence may come from the model, but much of the reliability comes from the architecture we build around it.

The final lesson may be the one that defined this period for us. None of this was happening on stable ground. The models were changing, the frameworks were changing, the infrastructure was changing and our own understanding of how to build agents was changing at the same time. We were already running production agents on MastraCloud Beta while Mastra itself was building the platform that would eventually become our production environment. We couldn't stop and wait for everything to mature. We had users, working agents and real problems to solve.

In hindsight, being forced to ride those waves was one of the best things that could have happened to us. We worked directly with the Mastra team, encountered problems neither side could have completely anticipated and solved them together. Every failure exposed another assumption. Every migration exposed another dependency. Every unexpected behavior taught us something about what production agent engineering actually required. We weren't simply migrating from Mastra Beta Cloud to Mastra Platform. We were learning how to operate production agents while the platform for operating production agents was evolving underneath us.

That experience changed my definition of production readiness. Production isn't the point where an agent stops changing. It is the point where you have enough engineering around it that it can continue changing without losing your ability to trust it.
