#### What is Graph Engineering?

I have to admit when I first saw the term being used I was suspicious. Graphs are so common in computing I thought to myself, what in the world could this nebulous term mean? The funny part is it can literally mean anything having to do with building and traversing graphs. I like to think graph engineering as both discovering relationships that would otherwise remain invisible and doing so at scale. In fact, with graphs you scale just about anything.

We have some of the best architects I've ever worked with at momoGood. I didn't build the brutal review agent, I didn't build the other stuff - I just built the graph.

The Ever Exapansive Universe

In the agentic universe an agent begins with a potentially enormous graph of information, entities and relationships. But the objective isn't to understand everything in the graph. It is to traverse it intelligently. Each traversal reveals new relationships. Those relationships provide signals about where to look next. The system retrieves, evaluates, narrows and traverses again. The graph may be expanding as new information is discovered, while the agent's attention is simultaneously converging on the small part of that graph that matters.

This is where intelligence begins to emerge in the middle. Intelligence isn't simply stored in the nodes. It comes from evaluating the relationships between them and deciding which relationships matter. Each traversal changes what the system knows and therefore changes where it should go next. The graph provides the structure through which intelligence can converge.

The valuable artifact isn't any individual node. It's the path. The path matters because not all relevance is the same. One piece of information may be semantically similar to what we're looking for. Another may contain an exact term or phrase. Another may be important because it is structurally connected to something we already know. These are different kinds of relevance, and increasingly I think the most capable systems will use all of them together. Semantic relevance asks what looks related. Lexical relevance asks what explicitly matches. Structural relevance asks what is connected. Graph engineering gives us the ability to reason across all three.

This is where graph based retrieval begins to look very different from searching a database or stuffing a pile of documents into a model. A traditional retrieval system might take a question, find the most relevant information, rank it and hand the result to the model. A more agentic system can do something much more interesting. It can investigate. It can retrieve something, inspect it, follow a relationship, discover another entity, reformulate what it is looking for, search again, reject a dead end, follow another edge, compare evidence and continue until it has enough confidence to act. The graph is no longer simply where information is stored. It becomes the search space, and the agent becomes the thing navigating it.

[grid]

That distinction matters because an agent may begin with access to millions of nodes and relationships, but only a tiny fraction of them belong in its working context at any particular moment. The problem isn't how to give an agent everything. The problem is how to help it discover the smallest useful subgraph that contains enough information and enough relationships to reason correctly. In that sense, graph engineering is also context engineering. The graph can be enormous while the context remains small. The system can leave most of the universe where it is and bring only the relevant stars into view.

A 2026 paper coauthored by Yuan Tian, [*Is GraphRAG Needed? From Basic RAG to Graph-/Agentic Solutions with Context Optimization*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25656), arrived at a remarkably similar conclusion from the retrieval side. The researchers compared regular RAG, GraphRAG, modular RAG and agentic RAG across nine scenarios. Their context engineering approach reduced token usage by 19 to 53 percent. But the finding I find more interesting is what they called the retrieval-generation gap. Retrieving more information did not produce a proportional improvement in the quality of the answer.

That is almost exactly the problem I had been circling from the other direction. Access to a larger graph is useful. Putting a larger graph into the context window is not necessarily useful at all. The job is not to retrieve everything that might be connected. It is to converge on the part of the graph that matters and represent it in a form the model can actually use. More retrieval can create more noise. Better traversal should create better context. The smallest useful subgraph is not a limitation of the system. It is the objective.

Much later I came across Anthropic's [Circuit Tracing](https://www.transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html) work and had one of those moments where I had to stop and stare at the screen. They were using attribution graphs to trace computational paths inside a language model. Our graph was outside the model, connecting code, tickets, people, messages and decisions. The domains were completely different. The shape of the problem was not. Start with an enormous graph. Identify the nodes that appear to matter. Follow the relationships between them. Prune what doesn't contribute. Inspect the path that remains.

I don't think one idea explains the other. That is what makes the parallel interesting. We arrived at graph traversal because our operational world had become too large to reason over all at once. Their researchers arrived there because a model's internal computation was too large to understand all at once. In both cases the useful thing was not the whole graph. It was the smallest defensible path through it.

This becomes even more interesting when the graph represents more than static documents. Think about a software organization. A production error is related to a service. That service is related to code. The code is related to a commit. The commit is related to a pull request. The pull request is related to a ticket. The ticket may be related to a customer request, a product decision, a Slack conversation and the people who made it. Now imagine traversing that graph backward from a production failure - error → service → code path → commit → pull request → ticket → decision → conversation. That isn't ordinary retrieval. It is reconstruction. The agent is rebuilding the chain of events and relationships that produced the current state of the system.

[fastlane]

This is one reason provenance becomes increasingly important. It isn't enough for an intelligent system to say that something appears to be true. We increasingly need to know why it believes that, what evidence supports it, where that evidence came from and how the pieces connect. A useful graph therefore doesn't just contain facts. It contains lineage. A claim can lead to evidence and evidence to a source. Code can lead to a commit, a pull request and ultimately a requirement. A decision can lead back to the conversation and people that produced it. The relationship itself becomes part of the evidence.

That gives us another interesting way to evaluate intelligent systems. We usually ask whether the agent reached the right answer, but graphs allow us to ask whether it followed a defensible path. Did it use reliable sources? Did it skip an important relationship? Did it arrive at the right answer through weak evidence? Did it traverse ten unnecessary nodes when three would have been sufficient? Did it manufacture a relationship that doesn't actually exist? Or did it discover a relationship we hadn't previously encoded? Those are very different questions from simply asking whether the final answer was correct, and they let us evaluate the quality of the reasoning path itself.

This is also where graphs can begin to improve themselves. Most graphs today are built because humans decided ahead of time which relationships matter. We define entities, write extraction logic, establish schemas and create edges. But an intelligent agent doesn't necessarily have to be limited to the relationships we anticipated. It can notice that two things appear related, form a hypothesis, search for evidence, test the relationship against other sources and establish some level of confidence. If the evidence is strong enough, it can create a new edge.

Now the graph isn't merely being traversed by intelligence. It is being shaped by intelligence. Retrieval leads to reasoning. Reasoning reveals relationships. Relationships improve the graph. The improved graph makes future retrieval better, and better retrieval creates better reasoning. That's a flywheel, and it's where I think graph engineering becomes something much larger than graph databases. It becomes a way of engineering an environment in which intelligence can discover, navigate, evaluate and eventually create relationships. Retrieval asks what information is relevant. Graph retrieval asks what information is connected. Agentic search asks which connection should be explored next. Graph intelligence asks what new relationship can be inferred from what has already been discovered.

Perhaps the most interesting question is what happens when the intelligence doing the searching can modify the very graph it uses to search. At that point we are no longer simply organizing information for machines. We're creating systems that can progressively organize the world for themselves. This is precisely what Tess and Jack do in Working Girl. Every connection they make changes the graph they are navigating and opens paths that weren't there before.
