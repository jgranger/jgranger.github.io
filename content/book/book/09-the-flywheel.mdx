# The Learning Flywheel

Artists are good at something that matters here. They start before they know what the finished thing looks like. You put something down, look at what you made, shape it, look again, shape it more. The first version isn't wasted just because it's not the final one. The shaping is the process.

That's a more honest description of how we actually built this ecosystem than anything that sounds like a roadmap. We didn't sit down with the finished agent architecture in mind. We built a small piece of functionality, put it in front of real people, watched what they did with it, and used what we learned to shape the next piece. Observe, infer, decide, act, and do it again with what you just learned folded in. That loop is the actual engine behind every agent in this book.

That's what makes it a flywheel instead of just a good workflow. Something gets built, people interact with it, those interactions generate signal, agents interpret the signal, the system gets smarter, and the next iteration starts from a better place. It takes a push to get moving. Once it's turning, each cycle makes the next one easier.

Speed still matters here, but not the reckless kind Life in the Fast Lane already warned about.  And that's the loop closing back to where this book started. The flywheel isn't separate from messaging - it's what messaging looks like once it's compounding. Something gets built, a message gets passed, people respond with messages of their own - questions, corrections, screenshots, complaints, praise - agents interpret those as signal, and the system gets better because it listened.

## The Flywheel in Motion

Every time an agent runs it has the potential to leave the system better than it found it. A question produces searches, searches produce paths through information, and those paths produce answers, decisions and actions. Humans and other agents evaluate the results, creating new signals about what worked, what failed, what was relevant and what should have happened instead. If we capture those signals they become fuel for the next turn of the wheel.

Use creates data, data creates feedback, feedback improves the system, and the improved system produces better outcomes the next time it runs. What I find particularly interesting is that the underlying model doesn't necessarily have to change for the system to become more intelligent. We can improve retrieval, discover new relationships in a graph, preserve useful knowledge, refine tools, strengthen evaluations, improve routing and learn which context matters for a particular objective. The model can remain exactly the same while the system surrounding it becomes progressively more capable.

Agents can also participate in turning the flywheel themselves. An agent can discover a gap, search for the missing information, evaluate what it finds and leave behind something useful for the next agent. That might be a new piece of knowledge, a relationship between two things, a successful search path or simply a signal that a particular approach didn't work. At scale, every interaction becomes an opportunity to improve the environment in which the next interaction occurs. The system isn't simply doing more work as the wheel accelerates. It is learning how to do the work better.

I later found almost the same idea showing up elsewhere. Anthropic's work on [long-running agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) leaves progress and artifacts behind for the next agent, and Eugene Yan describes finished work becoming context for whatever comes next in [How to Work and Compound with AI](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/working-with-ai/). That felt familiar. The work doesn't disappear when the task is done. If we build the system correctly, it becomes part of the next turn of the wheel.

### The Creative Act

The context we need to solve problems does not exist somewhere neatly packaged and waiting to be retrieved. We create it from the world around us by deciding what matters, what does not, how the pieces relate, and what we can do with them. As I'm writing this book one of my side reads is Rick Rubin’s The Creative Act. The book is built around a simple idea, which is creativity begins with how we experience the world. The artist pays attention, becomes sensitive to what is there, removes what gets in the way, and gradually gives something form. Creation is not separate from the world. It comes from our relationship with it.

**Creation begins with attention.** We notice what is already there.

**Creation requires exclusion.** We decide what belongs and what does not.

**Creation becomes connection.** Pieces that existed separately begin to take shape together.

**Creation becomes action.** What we have shaped becomes something we can act on and bring into the world.

**Creation becomes learning.** The action leaves something behind. A decision, an answer, a new relationship, a message from someone using what we built. That becomes part of the world we encounter the next time around. We pay attention again, but now there is more to notice. The creative act has changed the material of the next creative act.

There is no failure if what happened teaches us something about what to do next. Heidi Grant Halvorson calls this the Get Better mindset and I love her work, because it helped me shift my mindset prior to AI hitting the scene. Rather than approaching a problem trying to prove our worth and that we are capable of solving it, we approach it with the objective to learn. Her research shows that this change in mindset matters. People become more willing to persist through difficulty, learn from mistakes and explore rather than retreat when the answer is not immediately obvious. That opens the door to making connections we might otherwise miss and finding solutions we would not have found if our goal had simply been to be right the first time. 

This is exactly what has been happening throughout this book. Attention gives us something to learn from. Exclusion helps us find the signal. Connection gives us new possibilities. Action tests those possibilities against the real world. And learning is what comes back. The result of one creative act becomes the starting point for the next.
