To understand Context Engineering it helps to think visually. We'll borrow a term from the geospational industry where context really matters. The scenario I'm about to describe is near and dear to my heart. One of our momoGood team members lives in Spokane Washington. His family lost their home two years ago in some of the worst fires in state history. As I'm writing this in August 2026 Spokane just had the most destructive fire season ever recorded and over 1000 homes were lost.

Geospational software is used to monitor a defined surface area for specific objects or signals of interest. This area is often referred to as a bounding box. The larger the box the more area and events there are to monitor. Thus, the more noise there is to filter when responding a particular event. Why? Because the context almost always includes things both relevant and irrelevent to a particular event.

In reality a bounding box is a polygon and can take on just about any shape. A more realistic and meaningful example would look something like this.

<img src="/book-images/fires-2-1.png" width="1441" alt="" />

Responding to an event in a meaningful way requires proper context specific to that event. After all, it's the circumstances surrounding an event that give it meaning. For a response to be actionable having proper context is key. Much of signals intelligence is filtering out noise and focusing in on what's relevant to the operational response that will be forthcoming. The same is true for constructing proper context for solving just about any problem involving large amounts of data.

Think about the surface area of logs.

[pics]

Retrieval is one way we bring information into the model, but the model's context includes everything it has available to work with at that moment. Instructions, conversation history, tools, tool descriptions, retrieved documents, previous results and the artifacts produced along the way all compete for the same attention. Once I started thinking about context this way, the bounding box became even more useful. The problem isn't simply deciding what information to search. It is deciding what deserves to be inside the model's field of attention at all.

That attention is a finite resource. A larger context window gives us more room, but more room doesn't mean the model will use everything inside it equally well. Noise doesn't just take up space. It competes with the signal we're trying to isolate. This is why I think of context engineering as the continuous management of attention. We are shaping the surface area the model can see so that the things most important to the objective are easier to recognize and reason about.

This also changes the instinct to load everything up front. An agent doesn't need every document, every file and every tool result it might possibly use before it begins. It needs enough context to take the next intelligent step and the ability to retrieve more when the objective demands it. Anthropic describes this as [just-in-time context](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents), and it maps closely to what we discovered building our own agents. Instead of filling the context window in anticipation of every possible path, keep the larger world available and progressively disclose the parts that matter. Don't give the agent everything it might need. Give it the ability to find what it needs when it needs it.

The uncanny part is that the research keeps pointing in the same direction. Chroma's [Context Rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot) work found that performance became less reliable as context grew, and Eugene Yan found the same problem while studying [long-context question and answer systems](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/qa-evals/). Different paths, same signal. More context is not the same thing as better context. The model still has to find the signal. Operational context is our attempt to make that job smaller before asking the intelligence to do anything difficult.
### The Age of Retrieval

For roughly 25 years we had a question, we searched for it and got back ten blue links. Sometimes the answer was in the first one. Sometimes it took five searches and 12 browser tabs before we found what we were looking for.

Google did the fetching, but we still had a lot of heavy lifting to do. We filtered out what didn't matter. We figured out what was relevant. We compared one source against another, made connections between things that showed up in completely different places, and tried to make sense of what we found. Then we aggregated it, summarized it, made a decision and did something with it.

We were the context engine. Information was suddenly everywhere and remarkably easy to find, but turning it into something useful still required a tremendous amount of human effort. We got so used to doing that work that we barely noticed we were doing it.

We no longer live in a Google based galaxy. We no longer live in a world where humans must be the sole context engine. Yet, when we lived there we sure had a lot of heavy lifting to do. It was heavy, because we had to fetch, filter, reason, discern. Then aggregate, summarize, etc. The level of gravity was intense, and it required a commensurate amount of force to accomplish what now seems simple.

The Agentic Universe changes this. Searching is still important, but now it is one part of something much larger. An agent can search, look at what it finds, decide what matters, follow a connection somewhere else, realize it is missing something, search again and continue narrowing in on what it needs. It can make connections across different sources and use what it learns to do something.

In the Agentic Universe we're not constrained by the gravitational effects of one galaxy. The universe is ever expanding and there's room for us to expand along with it.

### RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation - before the model generates an answer, the system retrieves relevant material from an external source and inserts it into the model's context, so the answer is grounded in that retrieved content rather than generated from memory alone. With Rag, we retrieve what's relevant, let the model reason over it and get an answer. An agent goes further. It starts with an objective and figures out what context it actually needs, searches however many systems it takes, filters hard, reasons, acts, watches what happens, and interacts with the results.

We didn't set out to build a RAG system. We built a semantic search index over our codebase because live GitHub search couldn't scale, and a retrieval step because the agent needed grounded answers instead of guesses. Solving retrieval was the goal. RAG is just what that turned into.

The moment we indexed the code semantically and retrieved matching chunks to feed the model before it answered, we'd reconstructed the RAG pattern from first principles. We arrived at something as a natural overflow from evaluating our own needs.

### RAG++

RAG is powerful, but retrieval doesn't have to end with the first set of results. In the systems we're building, retrieval is the beginning of an investigation. An agent starts with an objective, searches for the most promising information, evaluates what it finds and uses those findings to decide where to look next. That search can branch across specialized agents working in parallel, each exploring a different source or relationship before returning what it learned to a coordinating agent. The important difference is that the context isn't predetermined. It emerges as the search unfolds. One discovery creates a reason to follow another edge, which leads to another source, which may change the search entirely. Rather than asking a model to reason over a static collection of retrieved chunks, we're giving the agent a graph it can progressively traverse and narrow until it has assembled the operational context necessary to answer the question. The goal isn't to retrieve everything that might be relevant. It's to discover the right path through the information that matters.

### Ask Product Agent Search

**Stage one, cosine similarity search**. The agent checks a library of answers people have already approved. If a new question is close enough by vector angle to one of those, it just hands back the approved answer. No reasoning needed. This is retrieval doing all the work.

**Stage two, sequential routing**. If nothing matches, the agent figures out what kind of question this actually is before doing anything else. Is it something to search for, something to escalate, or something off topic entirely. This step has to finish first, since there is no point launching a full search for a question that is about to get redirected instead.

**Stage three, RAG through parallel fanout**. Once it knows this is a real product question, the agent goes looking in several places at once. Documentation, product data, code, all searched at the same time instead of one after another. This is retrieval augmented generation in the proper sense, since none of what comes back is the final answer. It is raw material the agent still has to read, weigh, and write something new from. Speed comes from doing it all at once instead of standing in line.

**Stage four, graph based search**. This is the real standout. Inside the code search specifically, the agent is not just looking for text that looks similar. It is following actual relationships between pieces of code, what calls what, what depends on what, what is wired to what elsewhere in the system. It starts from its best matches and walks outward along those connections, the way you would follow one lead to the next while digging into something yourself.

**Stage five, dynamic agentic search**. This sits on top of everything else. After gathering all of that, the agent looks at what it has and makes a judgment call. If it is not enough, it does not settle. It runs another round of RAG on its own initiative, searching again and differently, without anyone telling it to. That willingness to keep digging until it actually has something solid is what separates this from a simple lookup tool.

More relevant results used to mean having a better shot at understanding something. Feed a model more of everything and you don't get better reasoning and often you get worse. Give the model everything it needs and nothing it doesn't. We got there by building the Ask Product agent against momoGood's own world, and that world is enormous; product knowledge, Shortcut stories, GitHub, production data, messages, documentation, every answer that's been approved before. The agent can search all of it.
### Attention Please

We call it context engineering, but what we're really competing for is attention. Apparently machines have finally developed the same problem we have.

There's another benefit to breaking investigation across agents that I didn't appreciate at first. Parallel agents aren't only a way to make the work faster. They are a way to isolate context. A specialized agent can go deep into one branch of an investigation, consume a large amount of information, discard what didn't matter and return only the useful result. The coordinating agent doesn't need to carry the entire investigation in its own context. It gets the signal without inheriting all of the noise required to find it.

This becomes even more important as agents run longer. Every search, tool call, observation and decision produces more context. Eventually the agent can create the same problem for itself that we were trying to solve with retrieval in the first place. It accumulates too much. Context engineering therefore can't be something we do once at the beginning of a request. The context has to be continuously shaped as the work unfolds. Useful findings can be preserved, completed work can be summarized, old details can be compacted and irrelevant branches can fall away. The agent's working context should evolve with the objective.

This is where the idea of operational context becomes much larger than RAG. Operational context is not a pile of relevant documents. It is the smallest useful working state that allows intelligence to take the next correct step. Sometimes that means retrieving another document. Sometimes it means following an edge in a graph, asking another agent to investigate a branch, calling a tool or discarding information that has already served its purpose. The context can expand while the agent is exploring and contract again as it learns what matters. The objective is not to keep everything. The objective is to keep the signal concentrated around the work that remains.

### **Does this Context Spark Joy?**

Marie Kondo built an entire philosophy around a deceptively simple idea. The goal isn't to organize everything you own. It is to decide what deserves to remain. Her famous test is whether something “sparks joy,” but the deeper principle is selection. Remove what doesn't matter and suddenly what does matter becomes easier to see.

<img src="/book-images/kondo-2.png" width="1250" alt="" />

Context engineering has a similar problem. We have become remarkably good at giving models more. Bigger context windows make it tempting to keep stuffing things into them simply because we can. But intelligence still has to separate signal from everything surrounding it. Sometimes the best thing we can do is clean the room.

There's a fascinating parallel here to the LIMA paper, [_Less Is More for Alignment_](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206?utm_source=chatgpt.com). Researchers took a 65-billion-parameter language model and fine-tuned it using only 1,000 carefully selected examples—without reinforcement learning or human preference modeling—and still produced remarkably strong results. Their conclusion became known as the **Superficial Alignment Hypothesis** - much of the intelligence was already there. The model didn't necessarily need more information. It needed the right information to activate and shape what it already knew.

Learning this changed how I think about context windows altogether. A larger context window gives us more room, but having more room doesn't mean we should fill it. The real engineering challenge is knowing where to point the intelligence and how far outward it needs to look. The Ask Product agent was the first agent that showed us intelligence gets a lot more useful once you hand it the right context before asking it to think.
### **Aim Small Miss Small**

<img src="/book-images/wild-thing.png" alt="" />

Our instinct is often to give an intelligent system everything we can find and hope it figures out what matters. More documents, more search results, larger context windows, more tools, more tokens. But every piece of additional context expands the space the model has to reason across. More isn't necessarily better. Sometimes more just means more places to miss.

The objective is **operational context**, the smallest, highest-signal neighborhood of information necessary to accomplish the objective. This doesn't mean starving the model of information. It means becoming much more deliberate about what information enters its field of attention. LIMA demonstrated something similar during alignment. A relatively small number of carefully selected examples was enough to shape the behavior of a model that had already learned an enormous amount about the world. At inference time, context engineering asks a related question. How little information can we provide while still giving the intelligence everything it needs to reason correctly?
