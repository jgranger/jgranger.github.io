Something happened after Ask Product had been in people's hands for a few weeks that I didn't expect. The people changed, their questions changed and their expectations changed. They learned what the agent was good at, where it needed more context and how to communicate with it in ways that produced better results. Two salespeople could use the same system in completely different ways and both make it work for them. One became more precise. The other kept using the shorthand and rhythm he already used with people. The agent had to meet both of them where they were.


 We had been thinking about learning mostly in terms of the agent. Was it getting better? Were the answers improving? Were retrieval, routing and evaluation improving? But the people using the system were learning too. The agent was changing the organization, and the organization was changing the agent. That feedback loop matters because it points to something much larger than better software.

## What the Work Leaves Behind

Every time an agent does useful work it has the potential to leave something behind. A good answer can become an approved answer. An investigation can leave evidence. A failed result can become an evaluation. A useful path through information can reveal a relationship that belongs in the graph. A tool can improve because we learned where people actually need it. This is where the flywheel becomes more interesting. Use creates information, which creates feedback that improves the environment. That's what makes the next interaction better. Over time the work itself can become part of the system's intelligence.

Organizations already have enormous amounts of intelligence scattered everywhere. It lives in people, conversations, documents, code, customer history, decisions, habits and relationships. Most of the time those things are disconnected. The knowledge exists, but it does not always show up at the moment it matters. Graph engineering gave me a different way to see that problem. We do not need to put everything into one giant context window. We need to make the right things discoverable and connected. We need to be able to move through the graph toward what matters.
## The Most Interesting Agent

The most interesting agent I'm building now is not a sales or product agent. It is me. Not a chatbot that sounds like me. Not a digital avatar. I mean something much more useful. An intelligence built around the things I know, the way I work, the relationships I rely on, the tools I use and the judgment I have accumulated over a lifetime. Something capable of extending those abilities into places I cannot always be. Every person already has a graph like this.

A salesperson has customers, relationships, market knowledge, instincts about timing, ways of framing an offer, trusted sources and a particular way of communicating. A manager has people, priorities, history, judgment and a sense for what deserves attention. An engineer has code, systems, architecture, debugging patterns and technical instincts. A designer has taste, references, patterns and a way of knowing when something feels wrong before anyone can explain why.

Most of that intelligence is difficult to scale because it is attached to one person. It lives in their head, their inbox, their habits, their conversations and the people they know. That is starting to change. Self replication is not copying a person. It is identifying the parts of your intelligence that are valuable and giving them reach. Your context becomes part of the system. Your tools become available to it. Your standards become evaluations. Your relationships become a graph it can navigate. Your judgment becomes something the system can learn to apply within boundaries you define.

The same ideas we used to build agents for products can be turned inward. Context engineering becomes deciding what your replicated intelligence needs to know. Graph engineering becomes connecting it to the people, information and systems that give you leverage. Tools allow it to act. Evaluations keep it aligned with your standards. The flywheel helps it improve through use. This is not about removing yourself from the work. It is about increasing the surface area of what you uniquely contribute.

That idea now has a very practical parallel in Anthropic's [Agent Skills](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills). A skill packages procedural knowledge, instructions and supporting resources so an agent can discover and load them when the work calls for them. The whole thing is built around progressive disclosure. The agent knows the capability exists without carrying all of it in working context all the time.

That is remarkably close to how I think about self replication. You do not need to pour your entire life into one gigantic prompt. You need to identify the capabilities that matter, give them structure, connect them to the right tools and information, and make them available when the objective requires them. Your knowledge becomes something the system can discover instead of something that disappears whenever you are not in the room.

A salesperson should be able to remain present across more accounts without becoming generic. A manager should be able to carry more organizational context without spending every waking hour in meetings. A designer should be able to extend their taste into work before it reaches review. An engineer should be able to keep investigating a system while their attention is somewhere else.

There is a warning here too. Scaling yourself is not the same thing as outsourcing yourself. Anthropic's [research on AI assistance and coding skills](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills) found that developers who used AI assistance in their experiment scored lower on mastery afterward. The important part is not that AI made people worse. It is that how we use the leverage matters. If the machine does all of the thinking that used to make us better, we can gain speed while quietly giving up the very judgment we were trying to scale.

That is why learning has to stay inside the loop. Let the machine search faster. Let it remember more. Let it investigate while your attention is somewhere else. But keep asking questions. Keep checking the path. Keep understanding why the answer is true. The point is to extend your intelligence, not to stop exercising it.

## Scale Yourself

What are you unusually good at? What do people come to you for? What information do you rely on? What relationships matter to the way you work? Which tools do you use without even thinking about them? What standards do you apply that other people may not even realize you have? What patterns do you notice because you have seen them a hundred times before? Those are not random personal details. They are parts of your graph.

Tess McGill never became more capable by becoming someone else. What made her unusually valuable was already there. Her curiosity, her instinct to keep looking, her ability to find connections between things other people had seen but had never put together.

But Tess alone wasn't enough. Jack Trainer could reach parts of the world she couldn't. He had relationships, experience, credibility and access. Tess could see connections Jack would never have looked for, and Jack could activate connections Tess could not reach on her own. Together they could move through a much larger graph than either of them could alone.

That is another way to think about scaling yourself. It doesn't mean making copies of yourself. Start with what you are unusually good at and give it reach. Connect it to people who are good at things you are not. Connect it to agents that can search, remember, investigate and act while your attention is somewhere else. Give those agents your context, your tools and your standards. Let the graph grow around what makes you valuable in the first place.

By the end of Working Girl, Tess has earned the opportunity to do things her own way. She doesn't simply step into a predefined structure, follow the procedure and execute the tasks someone else has given her. She brings herself to the work. Her curiosity, judgment and willingness to make connections are still there, but so is something that would have been easy to lose along the way. Her empathy.

Confidence and empathy are a remarkable combination. Tess learns to trust what she sees and act on it without losing her ability to see the people around her. That matters even more as we begin scaling ourselves with machines. There are things machines can search faster, remember longer and execute more consistently than we ever will. Let them. The opportunity isn't to become more like the machine. It is to use the machine to extend what is uniquely ours.

Scale your intelligence. Scale your reach. Scale your ability to make connections and act on them. But don't scale yourself by sanding away the qualities that made you worth scaling in the first place.

The question is no longer only what can agents do for a company. It is what could your own intelligence become if it were no longer limited to you. Once you see that possibility, the next question is unavoidable. How do you actually build it?

That is where the next journey begins.
